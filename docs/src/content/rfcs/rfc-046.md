## Context

[RFC-022](/rfcs/rfc-022) describes chronolexography: each legal fact is a dated entry, a chronolexogram, in the chronicle (*kroniek*) of the organization that establishes it. That organization is a cell (*cel*). RFC-022 keeps what a cell records out of the law: a chronicle-stream in a separate `chronicles/` store names the facts, who records them and on which ground (principle 2, §1.3), and integration configuration is no property of the law (principle 6). Only where it must touch a regulation does it go in a namespaced `extensions` block (§3.2).

A proof of concept used that fallback. It put a §3.2 `extensions` block on the regulation for each establishing article, naming the type of chronolexogram and its fields. A rulework then needs words a jurist does not use, and most of the block repeats what the law already says. Leerplichtwet 1969 art. 13a says that the head of a school grants leave "op aanvraag". [RFC-045](/rfcs/rfc-045) records that as `produces.submission: {kind: AANVRAAG}`, and the Awb hooks onto it. Art. 13a asks only what the application holds; what the head needs to decide comes with the decision. That is enough to know what an application for vakantieverlof contains.

## Decision

A rulework contains no chronolexography vocabulary. The law says what arises; the cell derives the record from it.

### 1. The test for a key

Can you explain the key to a jurist without the words chronolexogram, chronicle, cell or chronolex? Then it may stand in the rulework. Otherwise it belongs in the cell. `produces.submission: {kind: AANVRAAG}` passes: "this article establishes an application". `type: submission` with a list of fields does not.

### 2. What the cell derives for an application

| The law says | The cell derives |
|---|---|
| `produces.submission` on an article | That a gram arises, of that `kind`. The article is its `legal_basis` |
| a hook with `applies_to.submission` | That the hook's article takes part. The engine names these (`LawExecutionService::submission`) |
| a parameter of those articles | A field of the application. The cell refuses a name no article asks |
| `produces.moment` with a `parameter` | The moment that counts, under its own key `moment` in the gram. That parameter is the moment and no field of the application (RFC-045). The cell fills it with the day it records the application, passes it to the engine, and refuses it from the applicant. A moment parameter that is not a `date` is an error in the law |
| the same parameter in two of those articles | One field that rests on both. It can be absent only if both articles say so. A different type is an error in the law |
| `nullable: true` on a field the application leaves out | The field is absent ([RFC-036](/rfcs/rfc-036)). The gram holds `null` for it, as the engine receives it, so leaving the field out and sending `null` give the same gram. A field that cannot be absent stays unknown to the engine and out of `fields`; the gram names it under `missing`, because an incomplete application is still an application (Awb 4:5). The cell refuses `null` for it |

The cell executes the establishing article with the values supplied, so the hooks run, and records the outputs.

This derivation is the cell's own. Another runtime may record the same law differently.

One assumption in it is not in the law: the day of receipt (Awb 4:13 lid 1) is the day the cell records the application. The applicant does not supply it, and the cell has no intake that dates a letter on arrival. An application received on Friday and recorded on Monday therefore gets a deadline three days later than the law gives, and the chronicle's clock (§3) does not let the cell record it on Friday afterwards. A cell with an intake can let it supply the receipt date, no later than the day of recording.

### 3. The record

One application, received on 13 October 2026 by a fictional school:

```json
{
  "id": "019a1b2c-…",
  "kind": "AANVRAAG",
  "legal_basis": "leerplichtwet_1969#13a",
  "holder": "obs_de_klimop",
  "recorded_on": "2026-10-13",
  "moment": {
    "parameter": "datum_ontvangst",
    "on": "2026-10-13",
    "declared_by": "algemene_wet_bestuursrecht#4:13"
  },
  "fields": {
    "eerste_verlofdag": "2027-02-01",
    "naam_aanvrager": "J. Jansen"
  },
  "outputs": {
    "verlof_aangevraagd": true,
    "redelijke_beslistermijn_einddatum": "2026-12-08"
  }
}
```

The example leaves out most fields. The receipt is no field: `moment` holds its day, the parameter the engine received it in and the article that names it. When the law names no parameter, `parameter` is left out and the moment is the recording itself.

The gram does not repeat the law. Which articles ask a field, which article computed an output and which provision the moment rests on all follow from the law version in force on `recorded_on`, so the gram does not store them.

Against an entry of an RFC-022 chronicle-stream (§1.3), `holder` is its `recording_actor` and `legal_basis` its `grondslag`. The grounds of the separate values are not keys of the gram; they follow from that `grondslag` and `recorded_on`. A gram has no `intake`, because the cell has none (§2).

The chronicle is append-only and has one clock: a gram dated before the last one is refused. The same day is allowed.

## Why

### Benefits

- A rulework reads as law, without recording vocabulary.
- A change in the law changes the record. A new hook on every application adds its fields to every application gram.
- The cell has almost no configuration: who it is and where its chronicle lives.

### Tradeoffs

- The derivation is code in the cell, out of sight of the article. This RFC and the [Chronolexography](/concepts/chronolexography) page list the rules.
- A gram names its kind (`AANVRAAG`) and its article, not a name of its own. Two articles that establish an application are told apart by `legal_basis`.

### Alternatives Considered

**A block per establishing article** (the proof of concept). It worked, but it puts our vocabulary in the law, and nearly all of it can be derived.

**A field list in the cell's configuration, or a chronicle-stream in RFC-022's `chronicles/` store** (RFC-022 §1.3). Both keep recording vocabulary out of the law. Both also drift from it: a new Awb version that asks one more thing would not reach the record until someone edits the list.

**Per field and per output, the provisions it rests on** (an earlier draft of this slice, as `field_basis` and `output_basis`). It repeats the law in every gram, and a jurist checks those provisions in the law anyway (§3).

## Implementation

- `packages/cel` (`regelrecht-cel`): `shape::derive` reads the shape from the law through the engine; `Cell::record_submission` checks the values, executes the article and appends a gram; `Chronicle` keeps grams in memory or in a JSON Lines file.
- Not built: decisions on the application, reading the chronicle back, objections. Later RFCs add them.
